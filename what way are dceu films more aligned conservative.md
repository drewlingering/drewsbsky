  That's a much deeper question, because we can separate three things that are often conflated:

1. **The audience** (comic readers)

2. **The creators** (writers and artists)

3. **The stories themselves**

These can point in different political directions.
### Comic readers historically
Your observation about collecting is important.

The stereotypical comic-book reader from roughly the 1960s-1990s tended to be:

- Predominantly male.

- More intellectually engaged than average with fiction, continuity, history and mythology.

- A hobbyist and collector.

- Required some discretionary income.

- Often interested in science fiction, engineering, technology, gaming and fantasy alongside comics.

That demographic has never mapped neatly onto left-right politics.

For example:

- Engineers and technical professionals often lean economically centre-right but socially varied.

- Science-fiction fandom has traditionally contained a strong libertarian streak.

- Military enthusiasts, space enthusiasts and technology enthusiasts are common in comic readership.

- At the same time, comic readers often identify with outsiders, minorities and unconventional individuals, which can foster liberal attitudes.

Historically I'd argue mainstream comic readers were often **moderately libertarian or centrist**, rather than strongly conservative or progressive.
### DC's origins
DC's foundational heroes emerged in the 1930s-1940s.

**Superman creators**

- Jerry Siegel

- Joe Shuster

Both were children of Jewish immigrants.

Early Superman is surprisingly radical.

The Superman of 1938:

- Fights corrupt businessmen.

- Intimidates abusive employers.

- Battles political corruption.

- Challenges slumlords.

That is not modern conservatism.

If anything, early Superman resembles a New Deal-era populist reformer.

Batman meanwhile was less ideological. His core themes are:

- Personal responsibility.

- Order.

- Crime fighting.

- Self-mastery.

Those themes can be interpreted by conservatives, liberals and libertarians alike.
### Marvel's revolution
Marvel's breakthrough creators are especially significant.
#### Stan Lee
Stan Lee was broadly liberal by modern standards.

His famous "Stan's Soapbox" columns repeatedly argued against racism and prejudice.

He wrote in 1968:

> Bigotry and racism are among the deadliest social ills plaguing the world today.

His worldview was broadly:

- Humanistic.

- Tolerant.

- Optimistic.

- Internationalist.
#### Jack Kirby
Kirby is fascinating.

- Served in World War II.

- Strongly patriotic.

- Deeply anti-fascist.

- Suspicious of extremists.

- Believed in individual heroism.

Today both left and right claim Kirby.

In reality he was probably neither.

He resembles an old-school American liberal patriot.
#### Steve Ditko
One of the most politically distinctive comic creators ever.

Creator/co-creator of:

- Spider-Man

- Doctor Strange

Ditko became a devoted follower of Ayn Rand.

That places him much closer to:

- Libertarianism

- Objectivism

- Individualism

He believed strongly that:

- Reality is objective.

- Individuals are responsible for their actions.

- Government intervention should be limited.

Among major comic creators, Ditko is probably the furthest right economically.
### Alan Moore
An enormous influence on modern superhero storytelling.

Political outlook:

- Anarchist.

- Anti-authoritarian.

- Anti-corporate.

Works:

- Watchmen

- V for Vendetta

His influence pushed comics toward scepticism of power.

Ironically both left-wing and right-wing readers love Watchmen despite Moore's politics.
### Frank Miller
Another giant influence.

Early Miller:

- Libertarian.

- Anti-authoritarian.

Batman: The Dark Knight Returns became enormously influential.

After 9/11 he became more hawkish and security-focused.

His political evolution is one of the reasons modern Batman sometimes feels more conservative than Superman.
### Chris Claremont
The key architect of X-Men.

Politically:

- Progressive.

- Internationalist.

- Strongly interested in minority experiences.

X-Men has long been interpreted as:

- A metaphor for prejudice.

- A metaphor for minority rights.

This is one of the strongest progressive currents in mainstream comics.

### The surprising conclusion
If you look at the **foundational creators**, neither Marvel nor DC emerges as distinctly conservative.

Instead they were overwhelmingly created by people who:

- Distrusted concentrations of power.

- Opposed racism and fascism.

- Celebrated exceptional individuals.

- Believed ordinary people could improve society.

That's actually why both political camps find things to like in them.

A conservative reader sees:

- Self-reliance.

- Heroism.

- Duty.

- Courage.

A liberal reader sees:

- Inclusion.

- Social justice.

- Defence of the vulnerable.

- Resistance to oppression.

A libertarian reader sees:

- Suspicion of government.

- Individual freedom.

- Extraordinary individuals limiting state overreach.

The common thread isn't left or right.

It's almost a uniquely comic-book philosophy:

> **Power creates responsibility, and moral individuals must use power to protect others.**

That idea predates many of today's political divisions, which is why characters like Superman, Batman, Spider-Man and Captain America continue to attract audiences across the political spectrum even as the culture around them changes.
