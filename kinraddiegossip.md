That's an interesting reading, and it isn't entirely at odds with how some critics have interpreted [**Lewis Grassic Gibbon](https://outlook.live.com/people/0/Lewis+Grassic+Gibbon?EntityRepresentationId=c3ce02ab-fc75-8f16-89ef-b4f35cc92d02)**

In ***Sunset Song*** **(1932)**, gossip is everywhere in Kinraddie. The community constantly observes, judges, speculates about and talks about the lives of others. On one level, Gibbon uses gossip realistically to portray a small rural Scottish community. But it also serves a social and ideological function.

A Marxist reading might argue that gossip reflects the constraints of a traditional agrarian society, where people possess little economic or political power and therefore invest energy in the surveillance and regulation of one another's private lives. In that sense, gossip becomes an informal mechanism of social control.

Your suggestion goes a step further. Because fiction openly declares itself to be invented, it stands apart from gossip, which claims to relate real events and real people. One could argue that Gibbon's emphasis on gossip highlights a distinction between:

- **Fiction**, which openly acknowledges its imaginative nature.

- **Gossip**, which trades on claims about reality and social reputation.

There is also a class dimension. Marxist critics have sometimes viewed fascination with private scandal, personal reputation and individual morality as distractions from underlying economic and social relations. In *Sunset Song*, the villagers often discuss personal conduct while larger forces such as industrialisation, war, class relations and agricultural change reshape their world.

That said, Gibbon does not simply condemn gossip. He often uses it to reveal character, community values and the tensions between individual freedom and collective judgement. The novel's protagonist, Chris Guthrie, is repeatedly defined against the expectations and rumours circulating around her.

So a plausible interpretation is:

> Gibbon uses gossip not merely as local colour but as a way of showing how communities construct social reality. From a Marxist perspective, the focus on personal scandal and reputation can be read as a contrast to the deeper economic and historical forces that truly drive change, while fiction itself offers a more self-aware and reflective alternative to the truth-claims of gossip.

It's a less common reading than the usual discussions of gender, modernity, nationalism and class, but it's certainly defensible.
